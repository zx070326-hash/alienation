What Currency Buys Bandwidth for an Optical One-Way Street?

On-chip lasers have zero patience for back-reflections. Ordinary optics, meanwhile, insists on reciprocity — whatever goes forward can come right back. Building a broadband one-way street without bulky magnets turns out to be a surprisingly sharp accounting problem.

In one minute

Nonreciprocal bandwidth can only be bought with two currencies: mode count N in the lumped recirculating regime, or single-pass action crossing a threshold A* in the distributed regime. Once you cross A*, dispersion and colocated loss take over.

Why it matters

Ordinary optics obeys reciprocity. Breaking symmetry across a wide band without bulky magnets starts with knowing which tax physics is actually charging.

1. The real currency is frequency-wise Ω × dwell time

A common intuition is that broadband nonreciprocity is capped by total spectral weight. Exact accounting reveals something sharper: at each frequency ω, the separation Φ(ω) between the scattering matrix S and its transpose Sᵀ is bounded by the time-reversal-odd frequency Ω multiplied by the active dwell time trQₐ.

An intuitive mental model: earning a phase split

Picture forward and backward pulses racing in opposite directions along an active track: Ω is the differential rate, and trQₐ is how long light stays inside. When a single pass is too short (A < A*), light must loop inside a resonator to accumulate enough split — immediately trading away bandwidth.

That hard threshold A* splits linear nonreciprocal isolators into two distinct worlds:

  • Lumped / recirculating regime (A < A*): one pass is not enough, so light recirculates. Bandwidth is bought strictly with mode count N via B ≤ c* · N · Ω.
  • Single-pass / distributed regime (A ≥ A*): one pass earns the full split. Nonreciprocal physics stops capping bandwidth; instead, nonreciprocal dispersion, reciprocal dispersion, and colocated loss dominate.

3. A counter-intuitive lever: give the loss port delay

In a lumped isolator, it feels natural to dump rejected backward power into an instantaneous absorber. Yet exact network analysis shows the opposite:

  • For every FIR network with a zero-delay loss port (even without port symmetry), per-mode bandwidth cannot surpass the flat two-stage bound c_flat.
  • To surpass c_flat, the loss port itself must carry delay — routing rejected backward power through a delayed auxiliary path frees phase budget for flatter forward transmission.

That also explains why simply cascading single-mode magneto-optic rings hits a hard 2Ω wall: approaching the ceiling calls for multimode delay lines paired with delayed loss paths, not endlessly cranking up one cavity’s Q-factor.

4. The unavoidable tax: 1/ξ² vs. 1/ξ under colocated loss

Even above A* with flat dispersion, real nonreciprocal media carry colocated optical loss. Let ξ denote the ratio of nonreciprocal strength to loss. Every passive linear device obeys one universal inequality:

|S₂₁ − S₁₂|² ≤ 4 ξ² (1 − |S₂₁|²)(1 − |S₁₂|²)

At 20 dB isolation, this sets an absolute floor of 0.888 / ξ² dB, while a symmetric Mach–Zehnder interferometer (MZI) decays only linearly as 3.19 / ξ dB. Hover directly across the chart below (or drag the sliders) to feel how much laser power architecture alone can save.

5. Three lessons beyond photonics

  1. Identify the true currency before tuning parameters: Knowing whether you sit below or above A* immediately tells you whether to buy more modes or switch paradigms.
  2. Boundary ports are hidden design degrees of freedom: Treating the loss port as a delayed auxiliary channel — not an instantaneous dump — unlocks bandwidth beyond c_flat.
  3. Architecture can rewrite the scaling law itself (1/ξ → 1/ξ²): Breaking a symmetric-dwell assumption can cut loss by an order of magnitude without waiting for a 10× better material.
